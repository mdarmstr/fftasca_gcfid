\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract
Chemical separations data are typically analysed in the time domain using methods that integrate the discrete elution bands. Integrating the same chemical components across several samples must account for retention time drift over the course of an entire experiment as the physical characteristics of the separation are altered through several cycles of use. Failure to consistently integrate the components within a matrix of $M \times N$ samples and variables create artifacts that have a profound effect on the analysis and interpretation of the data. This work presents an alternative where the raw separations data are analysed in the frequency domain to account for the offset of the chromatographic peaks as a matrix of complex Fourier coefficients. We present a generalization of the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{permutation testing, and visualization}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{factorization, visualization, and permutation testing }\DIFaddend steps in ANOVA-Simultaneous Component Analysis (ASCA) to handle complex matrices, and use this method to analyze a synthetic dataset with known significant factors and compare the interpretation of a real dataset via its peak table and frequency domain representations.

\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:sample1}
\subsection{Challenges with peak table analysis}
Chromatographic separations enable detailed analyses of chemical mixtures, providing valuable information for researchers interrogating complex systems. For a multivariate analysis, several samples are collected and discrete chemical bands are integrated within time intervals whose apices are recorded as ``retention times'' - a descriptor of a chemical feature based on the elapsed time between injection and detection of the compound. This is typically done separately for each sample, and issues arise when the signal for each chemical feature is close to the baseline noise, or convolved with other closely-eluting chemical features. Within the multivariate context these challenges do become more significant\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, }\DIFaddend since a faithful representation of the chemical information must account for the fact that the same chemical features may drift between analytical runs, a phenomenon often referred to as ``peak drift''~\cite{christensen2005chromatographic}. Failure to integrate the same chemical features, or chromatographic peaks in the same column in an $M \times N$ matrix of experimental information (i.e., the ``peak table'') introduces artifacts that affect the resulting inferences from the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the }\DIFdelend analysis, since a peak may be falsely indicated as being distinct from similar chemical features in other samples (a false negative), or incorrectly associated with a chemical feature it is actually different from (similar to a false positive). In the former case, either zeros are introduced to an $n \in \{1...N\}$ column which is easy to notice from an inspection of the data, or possibly summed together with an unrelated feature which is much harder to identify. Both instances break the assumption of bilinearity within the context of a multivariate analysis, since these artifacts make covariance of the data poorly representative of the actual chemical phenomena being investigated \cite{armstrong2023parafac2,de2012integration}. 

Correlation Optimized Warping (COW) \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{tomasi2004correlation} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend and Ico-shift \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{savorani2010icoshift} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend are two examples of methods used for time series alignment for chromatographic and hyphenated separations data. COW optimizes the alignment of chromatographic or spectroscopic data by partitioning the signal into segments and warping each segment to maximize the correlation between the reference and sample signals, effectively correcting baseline shifts and peak \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{mis-alignments \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{trygg2007correlation}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{misalignments}\DIFaddend . On the other hand, Ico-shift employs an iterative \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, }\DIFdelend reference-free approach that automatically aligns \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{mass spectrometry data by minimizing the differences between spectra }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{signals by minimizing differences between samples }\DIFaddend through a series of incremental shifts, enhancing the precision of peak matching without the need for predefined reference points\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{schindler2015icoshift}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend . Both methods significantly improve data comparability and accuracy \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in the resulting analysis}\DIFaddend , facilitating more reliable analytical outcomes \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{in }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for }\DIFaddend complex datasets. These methods are useful for untargeted analyses, but may fail in instances where the shift exceeds the window specified by the parameters in the analysis. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{As such, care is required to optimize the parameters used in the alignment step prior to analysis \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{tomasi2004correlation}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
.
}\DIFaddend 

Although retention information alone is often used to integrate univariate chromatographic separations data samples, such as those from Gas Chromatography - Flame Induction Detection (GC-FID) experiments, hyphenated methods that connect a multivariate detector in sequence to separation step are often used to improve the identification of each chemical component. This information can not only be used to assist in the interpretation of the data, but also for the alignment of the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{data}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{chemical components themselves}\DIFaddend , because the detector itself is intrinsically aligned through \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{regular }\DIFaddend mass calibration in the case of a mass spectrometer and can be assumed not to vary significantly over the course of an experiment. While the raw data itself is multivariate, the chemical factors still suffer from their tendency to drift and sophisticated methods for integration are necessary to summarize the information as a peak table. Extensive research has been done into this type of data, owing to the popularity of High Performance Liquid Chromatography - Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS) data for the chemical analysis of bio-fluids in the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Omics field}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{various Omics fields}\DIFaddend . For Gas Chromatography - Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) data, an elegant approach for analysis can be performed using PARAFAC2 \cite{kiers1999parafac2}, which includes a relaxed constraint for trilinearity across \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$M \times J \times N$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$N \times M \times J$ }\DIFaddend acquisitions (i.e., measurements \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{(}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{that correspond to discrete }\DIFaddend retention times)\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{)}\DIFdelend , mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) and samples (later: replicates in the final peak table) respectively. PARAFAC2 cannot handle all problems with the analysis of hyphenated chromatographic data in one step \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{however; }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{- }\DIFaddend prior to analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) and their associated component numbers must also be selected \cite{baccolo2021untargeted,giebelhaus2022untargeted}. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{A tensor $\mathbf{X}_M \in \Re^{N \times M \times J}$ can be decomposed using the direct fitting method for PARAFAC2 decompositions following the expression which is optimized using Alternating Least Squares (ALS) for the $R$-rank sample-wise loadings $\mathbf{D}_M \in \Re^{R \times R \times M}$, spectral loadings $\mathbf{A} \in \Re^{J \times R}$,  and an orthogonal Procrustes step to optimize for the elution profiles in $\mathbf{P}_M \in \Re^{N \times R}$:
}

\begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:pf2}
    \mathbf{X}_M = \mathbf{P}_M\mathbf{H}\mathbf{D}_M\mathbf{A}^T +\mathbf{E}_M.
}\end{equation}

\DIFadd{Matrix $\mathbf{H} \in \Re^{R\times R}$ is an additional matrix used to describe the elution profiles that is optimized using an iteration of the PARAFAC algorithm along with all terms save for $\mathbf{P}_M$. $\mathbf{E}_M \in \Re^{N \times M}$ is the error term encompassing variation not accounted for by the model. The orthogonality constraint for $\mathbf{P}_M$ can be relaxed following a coupled decomposition which relates a least-squares solution for the elution profiles to the orthogonal solution through an additional regularization term \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{cohen2018nonnegative}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
, and several regularization terms can be added for additional chromatographic modes \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{armstrong2023parafac2}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
.
}

\DIFaddend While metabolomics experiments are rarely run on first-order instrumentation given the widespread availability of hyphenated methods, the complexity of the analysis (e.g. the mass spectral match factor for associating peaks between samples \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{as an additional alignment parameter}\DIFaddend ) also increases, which highlights the need for more parsimonious methods for handling the data across all possible modalities \cite{smith1995handbook}\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. Mass spectral match in particular varies as the signal dips closer to the detection limit of the instrument, and skewing is known to occur for scanning instruments like quadrupoles \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{samokhin2018spectral}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
.
}\DIFaddend 

Comparing strategies for the integration of chromatographic data (or ``pre-processing'' as it is commonly called), shows that the methodology used to extract the chemical features can drastically affect the results of a multivariate analysis~\cite{weggler2021unique}. And the best method is difficult to select for any problem when ``ground truth'' is unknown, as is common for all unsupervised learning.

This work suggests that an abstraction of the raw chromatographic data through a frequency domain representation can be used to subvert many common issues with chromatographic data analysis. This is done using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) on each sample within a designed experimental dataset collected using a Gas Chromatography Flame-Induction Detector (GC-FID) system followed by subsequent Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) - Simultaneous Component Analysis (ASCA)~\cite{smilde2005anova} that has been generalized to account for complex matrices. This technique recovers similar information to the more traditional peak table representation of the data, as provided by the authors of the original study, but requires an extra step to recover informative representations of the data in the time domain for the sake of interpreting the results. This is a simpler implementation of a method for analysis that is becoming widespread as an intermediate step in many newer algorithms ~\cite{schneide2023shift,schneide2024shift,yu2023parasias} that handle similar problems.

% Herein, an abstraction of the raw chromatographic data in the frequency domain is analysed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) - Simultaneous Component Analysis (ASCA)~\cite{smilde2005anova} via the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). This technique is used as an intermediate to solving problems with peak drift~\cite{schneide2023shift,schneide2024shift,yu2023parasias}, but this method analyses the frequency representation directly, and based on the factorization of the different experimental factors the data is interpreted through an inverse transform \textit{post-hoc}.

\subsection{ANOVA-Simultaneous Component Analysis}\label{sec:glm}

The following description of ASCA is summarized as per \cite{camacho2023permutation}. A matrix of experimental observations, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\mathbf{X} (N \times M)$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbf{X} (M \times N)$ }\DIFaddend can be factorized according to its encoded factor matrix, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\mathbf{D} (N \times F)$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbf{D} (M \times F)$ (where F is the total degrees of freedom specified by the levels of the experimental design) }\DIFaddend according to:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{D} \mathbf{\hat{\Theta}}^T + \mathbf{E}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbf{E}$ is an error matrix of the same dimensions as $\mathbf{X}$ that indicates the variance not accounted for by the model $\mathbf{D} \mathbf{\hat{\Theta}}^T$. $\mathbf{\hat{\Theta}}$ is typically calculated through a least-squares regression commonly referred to as a General Linear Model, which is equivalent to a standard ANOVA \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{or ASCA }\DIFdelend based on averages when the experimental design is balanced, and provides some robustness against unbalanced experiments when it is not \cite{smilde2005anova}. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{When GLMs are used, ASCA is typically referred to as ASCA+ to highlight this distinction, but for the remainder of this communication, ASCA will be used to refer to ASCA+ as implemented in the MEDA Toolbox used for this work \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{camacho2015multivariate}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. The regression of the real-valued experimental design matrix, $\mathbf{D}$ against the complex-valued $\mathbf{X}$ is realized through the complex conjugate and transpose of the regression coefficients $\mathbf{\Theta}$ in FFT-ASCA as described in Section \ref{sec:fftasca}.  }\DIFaddend The significance of each experimental factor, as a subset of the columns in $\mathbf{D}$ is typically performed using permutation testing:

\begin{equation}\DIFaddbegin \label{eq:perm1}
\DIFaddend p = \frac{\#\{\pi:F^\pi_{\nu_1,\nu_2} \geq F^{A}_{\nu_1,\nu_2}\} + 1}{\Pi+1}
\end{equation}

\noindent which for each permutation the calculated \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{F-ratio }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$F$-ratio }\DIFaddend ($F^\pi_{\nu_1,\nu_2}$) of the permutated data is compared against the nominal \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{F-ratio }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$F$-ratio }\DIFaddend of the un-permuted data ($F^{A}_{\nu_1,\nu_2}$) in order to simulate an empirical null distribution against which the significance of each experimental factor can be compared\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. The F-ratios }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, as the }\textit{\DIFadd{frequency}} \DIFadd{by which the $F$-ratio calculated on the permutated data, exceeds the $F$ ratio calculated on the un-permuted data. The $F$-ratios }\DIFaddend are calculated as a function of the sum of squares (SSQ) of the data reconstructed according to some subset ($\mathbf{D}_A\mathbf{\hat{\Theta}}_A^T$) of $\mathbf{D}\mathbf{\hat{\Theta}}^T$ for an arbitrary factor, $A$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
    \mathbf{X}_A = \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mathbf{C}}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mathbf{D}}\DIFaddend _A\mathbf{\hat{\Theta}}^T_A,
\end{equation}

\noindent against the residual matrix $E$\footnote{Note that other \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{F-ratios }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$F$-ratios }\DIFaddend can be used \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, }\DIFdelend in particular in the presence of random/nested effects and interactions \cite{anderson2014permutational}.}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, the }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{. The }\DIFaddend calculated test statistic $F_A$ for factor $A$ normalized \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for }\DIFaddend $\nu_1, \nu_2$ degrees of freedom:

 \begin{equation}\label{eq:sig_test}
    F^A_{\nu_1,\nu_2} = \frac{||\mathbf{X}_A||_F^2/\nu_1}{||\mathbf{E}||^2_F/\nu_2}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the squared Frobenius norm, denoted as $||\cdot||^2_F$, is used to indicate the sum of squares for the factor and residual matrices. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{For complex-valued data, the real-valued sum of squares are calculated through the following identity:
}%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\begin{displaymath}\DIFdel{%DIFDELCMD < \label{eq:ssq}%%%
    SS_A = ||\mathbf{X}_A||^2_F = Tr(\mathbf{X}_A\mathbf{X}_A^H)
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < \noindent %%%
\DIFdel{where $\mathbf{X}^H_A$ is the }\textit{\DIFdel{Hermitian}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{(i.e. conjugate transpose) of $\mathbf{X}_A$ and must be explicitly calculated to ensure that a real-valued scalar is returned. Proof of this identity is widely-known, and can be derived by taking the matrix product of any complex data and its complex conjugate.
}%DIFDELCMD < 

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
\DIFdelend Altogether these reconstructed matrices are nominally orthogonal in the case of a balanced design, and can be represented as summation of their contributions to the overall matrix of experimental data $\mathbf{X}$:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{1}\mu\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{^T }\DIFaddend + \mathbf{X}_A + \mathbf{X}_B + \mathbf{X}_{AB} + \mathbf{E},
\end{equation}

\noindent and the face-splitting product of the columns representing the linear factors themselves (such as $A$ and $B$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in $\mathbf{D}$}\DIFaddend ) can be used to calculate the interactions between the factors $AB$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. $\mu$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{as a additional subset of the columns within the design matrix. $\mathbf{1}\mu^T$ }\DIFaddend is the $1\times N$ vector of variable-wise means. These values subscript the reconstructions of each matrix, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$X_{A,B,AB...}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbf{X}_{A,B,AB...}$ }\DIFaddend as nominally orthogonal \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{reconstructions of the data }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{subspaces within the data (in the case of balanced designs)}\DIFaddend , spanning the experimental characteristics described by the similarly subscripted design matrix \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\mathbf{C}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbf{D}_{A,B,AB...} \in \mathbf{D}$ }\DIFaddend in Equation \ref{eq:rec}. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Further details about calculating the scores and loadings for each of the factorized reconstructions are given in Section \ref{sec:fftasca} within the context of the proposed }\textit{\DIFadd{FFT-ASCA}} \DIFadd{methodology.
}\DIFaddend 

\subsection{Fast Fourier Transforms}

A key characteristic of the pre-processing strategy proposed in this work involves a frequency domain representation of the raw experimental data. This is done using a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), which is an algorithmic acceleration of the Discrete Fourier Transform \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{representing }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(DFT) which equivalently represents }\DIFaddend the time-domain data as a series of complex coefficients. These coefficients encode the relative magnitude and offset of a series of sinusoidal components that sum to a least-squares representation of the original data, which may not be exact for discontinuous instances \cite{nussbaumer1974gibbs}\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, which are poorly represented by smooth sinusoidal functions}\DIFaddend . FFTs can be performed in $L$ modes \cite{smith1995handbook}, which may generalize to multi-modal separations and/or hyphenated data, but for the sake of demonstration only 1-dimensional transforms on univariate data will be considered. Take the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{1-dimensional }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{uni-dimensional }\DIFaddend discrete Fourier transform of a univariate time signal, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$x_m$, for $m \in \{1...M\}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$x_n$, for $n \in \{1...N\}$ }\DIFaddend acquisitions in the time domain:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fft}
    x_k = \sum_{m=0}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{^{M-1} }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{^{N-1} }\DIFaddend x\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_m }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_n }\DIFaddend e\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{^{-i \frac{2\pi}{M} km}}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{^{-i \frac{2\pi}{N} kn}}\DIFaddend \textnormal{, for } k \in \{1...\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{M}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{N}\DIFaddend /2\}
\end{equation}

\noindent where the right hand side of Equation \ref{eq:fft} transforms the data into $k$ frequencies up to \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$M/2$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$N/2$}\DIFaddend , or half the total number of acquisitions where \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$M/2$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$N/2$ }\DIFaddend is the limiting \textit{Nyquist} frequency \cite{smith1995handbook}. The relationship is linear, and can be inverted:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ifft}
    x\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_m }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_n }\DIFaddend = \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\frac{1}{M} }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\frac{1}{N} }\DIFaddend \sum_{k=0}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{^{M-1} }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{^{N-1} }\DIFaddend x_k e\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{^{i \frac{2\pi}{M} kn}
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{^{i \frac{2\pi}{N} kn}
}\DIFaddend \end{equation}

Note that the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{conventions for }\DIFdelend use of the normalization constant \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\frac{1}{M}$ for }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\frac{1}{N}$ in }\DIFaddend the forward or inverse \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{transforms vary by definition, since for equi-spaced datathe }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Fourier transform depends on the specific convention adopted. \DIFaddbegin \subsection{\DIFadd{FFT-ASCA}}\label{sec:fftasca}

\DIFadd{For complex-valued data, the real-valued sum of squares are calculated through the following identity:
}

\begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:ssq}
    SS_A = ||\mathbf{X}_A||^2_F = Tr(\mathbf{X}_A\mathbf{X}_A^H)
}\end{equation}

\noindent \DIFadd{where $\mathbf{X}^H_A$ is the }\textit{\DIFadd{Hermitian}} \DIFadd{(i.e. conjugate transpose) of $\mathbf{X}_A$ and must be explicitly calculated to ensure that a real-valued scalar is returned. Proof of this identity is widely known and can be derived by taking the matrix product of any complex data and its complex conjugate.
}

\DIFaddend In this case, for a series of time-series data representing \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$N$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$M$ }\DIFaddend chromatographic samples, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$x_m$, }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{each $x_n$ vector }\DIFaddend is indexed row-wise \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(i.e. the total number of acquisitions per sample) }\DIFaddend and each are transformed into the frequency domain via Equation \ref{eq:fft}. An ASCA analysis is then performed on the matrix of the complex coefficients - where statistical significance testing is performed on the magnitude of each complex coefficient according to Figure \ref{fig:imag}, and Equation \ref{eq:ssq}.

Significance testing \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{follows as normally, and the complex characteristics of the data are preserved for the subsequent visualization step. The loadings of the reconstructed matrices }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{proceeds by measuring the relative magnitude of the total variance encapsulated by the factorized data, over the residual variance to yield $F^{A,\mathbb{C}}_{\nu_1,\nu_2}$ for some factor $A$:
}

\begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:sig_test_comp}
    F^{A,\mathbb{C}}_{\nu_1,\nu_2} = \frac{Tr\left(\mathbf{X}_A\mathbf{X}_A^H\right)^2/\nu_1}{Tr\left(\mathbf{E}\mathbf{E}^H\right)^2/\nu_2}\
}\end{equation}

\noindent \DIFadd{and the frequency with which the magnitude of the real-valued $F$-ratios calculated with the permuted data ($F^{\pi,\mathbb{C}}_{\nu_1,\nu_2}$)  exceed the nominal values ($F^{A,\mathbb{C}}_{\nu_1,\nu_2}$) is calculated as in Equation \ref{eq:perm1}:
}

\begin{equation}\DIFadd{\label{eq:perm2}
p = \frac{\#\{\pi:F^{\pi,\mathbb{C}}_{\nu_1,\nu_2} \geq F^{A,\mathbb{C}}_{\nu_1,\nu_2}\} + 1}{\Pi+1}
}\end{equation}

\noindent \DIFadd{for the total number of permutations: $\pi \in \Pi$. The complex loadings and scores of the resultant ASCA model }\DIFaddend for each experimental factor are \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{projected using summed reconstruction and error terms to calculate the scores for }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{calculated by performing Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on each of the reconstructed matrices, as a Simultaneous Component Analysis (SCA). To compute }\DIFaddend the ASCA model \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{according to an arbitrary }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{scores for a given experimental }\DIFaddend factor $A$\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, the complex-valued loadings of the reconstructed matrices are projected by summing the reconstructed data along the associated error terms}\DIFaddend :
%
\begin{align}\label{eq:asca}
    \mathbf{T}_A\mathbf{P}_A^H = \mathbf{X}_A \\
    \mathbf{T}^*_A = (\mathbf{X}_A + \mathbf{E})\mathbf{P}_A
\end{align}
%
\noindent where the scores and loadings matrices $\mathbf{T}_A$ and $\mathbf{P}_A$ are respectively \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$N \times R$, and $M \times R$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$M \times R$, and $N \times R$ }\DIFaddend complex matrices, with $\mathbf{P}_A^H$ representing the Hermitian of the loadings matrix $\mathbf{P}_A$. In the frequency domain, the loadings $\mathbf{P}_A$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{may not be }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are not }\DIFaddend particularly easy to interpret as an abstraction of the original data in the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{time series}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{frequency domain}\DIFaddend . But the \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{complex-valued }\DIFaddend loadings can be transformed back into the time domain \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{without sacrificing the least-squares solution to the GLM-like (General least squares, similar to ANOVA under certain conditions - see Section \ref{sec:glm}) problem as in }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for inspection using }\DIFaddend Equation \ref{eq:least_squares}. Consider \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\mathbf{X}_m$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathbf{X}_n$ }\DIFaddend as the time-domain representation of a designed experimental dataset with one significant factor, and $\mathbf{X}_k$ as the corresponding frequency domain representation. Equations \ref{eq:fft} and \ref{eq:ifft} can be represented by the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$M\times M$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$N\times N$ }\DIFaddend linear transformation $\mathbf{F}$ for the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\frac{1}{M}\mathbf{F}^{-H} = \mathbf{F}^{-1}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\frac{1}{N}\mathbf{F}^{-H} = \mathbf{F}^{-1}$ }\DIFaddend for the corresponding inverse scaled by the number of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{observations, $M$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{acquisitions, $N$}\DIFaddend . It follows that:

\begin{align}\label{eq:least_squares}
    \mathbf{X}_k = \mathbf{X}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_m}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_n}\DIFaddend \mathbf{F}\\
    \mathbf{X}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_m }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_n }\DIFaddend = \mathbf{X}_k\mathbf{F}^{-1}\\
    \mathbf{X}_k = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^H + \mathbf{E}\\
    \mathbf{X}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_m }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_n }\DIFaddend = \left(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{P}^H + \mathbf{E}\right)\mathbf{F}^{-1}\\
    \mathbf{X}\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{_m }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{_n }\DIFaddend = \mathbf{T}\left(\mathbf{P}^H\mathbf{F}^{-1}\right) + \mathbf{E}\mathbf{F}^{-1}
\end{align}

\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{And }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \noindent \DIFaddend so the transform of the loadings from the frequency domain \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{encompass }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{encompasses a }\DIFaddend similar variance as a function of the linear transformation $\mathbf{F}$, and can be interpreted following the inverse transform. Additionally, the reconstructions of $\mathbf{X}$ according to the different experimental factors may be transformed back to the time domain for an inspection of the different peaks responsible for affecting the level separations. Inspecting the equations shows that the relationship is \textit{least squares} in the frequency domain, which is not to say the same relationship holds in the time domain (in instances where there is significant peak drift). \DIFaddbegin \subsection{\DIFadd{Correlation Optimized Warping (COW)}}

\DIFadd{Time-series data with drift between observations can be aligned using traditional chemometric techniques, such as dynamic time warping (DTW)\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{wang1987time} }\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
or correlation-optimized warping (COW)\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{nielsen1998aligning}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
. For the sake of simplicity, only COW will be discussed and compared with the proposed frequency-domain analysis of the chromatographic data.
}

\DIFadd{COW is a dynamic programming implementation that ``stretches'' or ``compresses'' each segment, for all samples according to the interpolation that best fits the corresponding reference sample, for each interval that either expands or contracts according to the ``slack'' parameter. This is done to maximize a correlation coefficient (such as the Pearson correlation coefficient) for each segment, although this coefficient can also be raised to higher powers where the optimum is the best trade-off for preserving the natural variation between samples over emphasizing the need for agreement with the reference sample. The optimal parameters for slack and interval size can be found using a grid search \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{skov2006automated, tomasi2004correlation}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
.
}

From the results of Table \ref{tb:results} it is clear from the apparent significance of the experimental factors, that the presence of zeros and/or missing data has a profound effect on the resulting interpretation of the data. In the peak table representation, failure to account for zeros neglects to demonstrate the significance of the time factor, and suppresses the relative weight of the interacting term. Because the peak table representation relies on an intermediate step to integrate the peaks, it is impossible to know which zeros are truly absent from the data, and which zeros have failed to be integrated by the software. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{However it }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{It }\DIFaddend is worth noting that a similar treatment in the time and frequency domains yields \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{similar results }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{identical results for the inference of significant factors }\DIFaddend when the zeros are accounted for by the permutational cell mean replacement routine. In cases where the chemical factors are truly absent from one class, they are nonetheless replaced with the average, or expected value according to samples with similar experimental characteristics. The \textit{p}-values are driven by the frequency with which the simulated \textit{null} hypothesis exceeds the apparent test statistic, which is in turn driven by the difference between experimental levels over variation within samples with similar characteristics. And so for instances where a chemical component is truly absent from one set of samples, whether or not the value is represented by zero, or the imputed value per \textit{pCMR} (permutational cell mean replacement) is not expected to be particularly consequential if the difference is continually observed over several permutations of the original data \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{merchanskaya2024considerations}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend .

Figures \ref{fig:figure1} and \ref{fig:figure2} demonstrate that the scores from the analysis appear to be more consistent using FFT-ASCA than by using ASCA on the peak table information alone\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, and the }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{; while pCMR is currently only capable of handling issues with significance testing, visualization is still strongly influenced by the presence of missing data. The }\DIFaddend discrete peak loadings in Figure \ref{fig:figure5} show some similarity to the continuous, inverse transformed loadings \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{of the raw data }\DIFaddend in Figure \ref{fig:figure6} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. \section{Conclusions}

FFT-ASCA analyses appear to subvert some common problems with pre-processing of chromatographic data. In the experimental dataset, the presence of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{missing values }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{zeros }\DIFaddend in the peak table was shown to have a strong effect on the resulting interpretation. A transformation of the raw data itself \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{however }\DIFdelend to a series of fully-populated Fourier coefficients offers similar insight into the analysis following missing value correction, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and for a similar }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for a comparable }\DIFaddend level of interpretability. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{When zeros are treated as missing values in the peak table, and imputed - the results are similar to the analysis performed using FFT-ASCA. However, when the zeros are not imputed - the resulting statistical inference is quite different. The zeros in the peak table may not necessarily indicate missing values. Instead, they reflect the inherent uncertainty in peak table analyses. It is often unclear whether these zeros accurately represent the absence of certain chemical factors or if they result from the software's inability to consistently integrate peak information. This ambiguity complicates the interpretation of the data. As such, in the absence of the ground truth regarding the experimental data, whether to use missing value imputation or simply trust that the zeros are properly representative of the underlying chemical information - remains unknown for the peak table analysis.
}

\DIFaddend While simple, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{this method }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{FFT-ASCA }\DIFaddend offers new ways of analyzing chromatographic data, in a manner that relies on fewer meta-parameters for feature engineering and extraction prior to the analysis step. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{In particular, arbitrary parameters for the COW alignment appear to suffer from similar problems to do with peak drift, that may be addressed by optimizing the parameters for the level of drift at-hand. This optimization procedure does however run the risk of converging to sub-optimal solutions and returning a result that is poorly representative of meaningful variation in the data, as demonstrated by the comparison of techniques on synthetic data. }\DIFaddend Although this technique has not been definitely proven to out-perform traditional methods for analyzing chromatographic data in all cases, this demonstration is representative of a number of issues faced by laboratory scientists handling and interpreting chromatographic data. GC-FID is not commonly used for metabolomics-type work, but this type of methodology is easily generalized to hyphenated and/or multi-modal separations using similar extensions of multivariate statistics to tensor analysis that have already been widely discussed in literature \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{koleini2023complementary}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{\mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\cite{koleini2023complementary,armstrong2024replicate}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFaddend .
